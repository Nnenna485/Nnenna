## [0:00] The empire on which the sun never set

In 1922, wherever you stood on Earth and watched the sun come up, a British flag somewhere under that same sun was already flying.

Canada. Jamaica. Nigeria. India. Australia. Tiny rocks in the middle of the Pacific.

Nearly one in every four people alive answered, on paper at least, to a king in London.

And the strangest part? It all started on a wet, middling island off the coast of Europe that, for most of its history, nobody thought mattered very much.

This is the entire history of the British Empire. How a country smaller than Oregon came to rule a quarter of the planet, what it took to hold it, and how it lost almost all of it in a single lifetime.

## [0:55] An island on the edge of the map

In 1497, the map of the world had a center, and it wasn't England.

Spain and Portugal had split the newly discovered oceans between them, with the Pope's blessing. England was a poor, peripheral kingdom with a small navy and no colonies at all.

That year, an Italian sailor named John Cabot, sailing for King Henry the Seventh, reached the coast of North America, probably Newfoundland. He found fish so thick, it was said, you could haul them up in baskets.

He found no gold. England more or less forgot about it.

For the next century, England's real overseas strategy was much simpler: steal from Spain. Queen Elizabeth the First licensed privateers, state-approved pirates like Francis Drake, to raid Spanish treasure fleets.

In 1588, Spain sent a massive Armada to end the problem. English fire ships and North Atlantic storms scattered it.

England hadn't become a great power. But it had learned the lesson that would shape the next four hundred years: an island that controls the sea is very hard to touch.

## [2:10] Tobacco, sugar and chains

In 1607, about a hundred English settlers built a fort on a swampy river in Virginia and named it Jamestown. Within a few years, most of them were dead.

The colony survived for one reason: tobacco. Europe could not get enough of it.

Further south, in the Caribbean, the English found something even more profitable. Barbados, settled in 1627, and Jamaica, seized from Spain in 1655, became sugar islands.

And sugar needed labor that no one would ever volunteer for: brutal, deadly work in the heat of the cane fields.

So Britain became one of the largest slave traders in history. British ships carried more than three million enslaved Africans across the Atlantic. The profits flowed back to Bristol and Liverpool, into banks, country houses and the first factories.

This was the first British Empire: Atlantic, commercial, and built on the bodies of people who never appeared on its maps.

In 1707, England and Scotland united into a single kingdom: Great Britain. The Scots had just lost a fortune on a failed colony of their own in Panama, and now they threw themselves into the empire as soldiers, merchants, doctors and governors.

## [3:40] A company with an army

Meanwhile, on the last day of 1600, Queen Elizabeth had signed a charter for a small trading business: the East India Company. It wanted spices, then cotton, silk and tea. It was a company, not a country.

India at the time was ruled by the Mughal Empire, one of the richest states on Earth. The Company was a guest, renting warehouses in port towns like Madras, Bombay and Calcutta.

But in the 1700s, Mughal power began to fracture. And the Company did something no business should ever be allowed to do: it raised its own army. By the early 1800s it had around 200,000 soldiers, about twice the size of the British Army.

In 1757, at a place called Plassey, Company troops under Robert Clive defeated the Nawab of Bengal, helped by the fact that the Nawab's own commander had been bribed to stand aside.

Eight years later, the Mughal emperor handed the Company the right to collect taxes across Bengal.

Think about that. A private company, with shareholders in London, now ran one of the wealthiest regions in the world. When famine struck Bengal in 1770 and killed, by some estimates, a third of its people, the Company kept collecting.

## [5:00] Losing America, finding Australia

In 1763, Britain won the Seven Years' War against France, the first truly global war, fought from Canada to India. Britain took Canada and Florida, and found itself with an enormous empire and an enormous war debt.

So Parliament did what seemed logical: it asked the American colonists to help pay for their own defense.

The colonists did not see it that way. No taxation without representation. First stamps, then tea, then muskets. By 1783, Britain had lost thirteen of its most valuable colonies.

It should have been the end. Instead, it was a pivot. Britain turned east and south.

It had been shipping convicts to America. Now it needed somewhere new to send them. In January 1788, eleven ships, the First Fleet, dropped anchor in Sydney Cove on the far side of the world.

For the Aboriginal peoples who had lived on that land for tens of thousands of years, it was the beginning of an invasion. For Britain, it was the beginning of a second empire.

## [6:15] Rule the waves

In 1805, at Trafalgar, Admiral Nelson destroyed the combined fleets of France and Spain. In 1815, Napoleon was finally beaten at Waterloo.

Britain came out of those wars with the most powerful navy on the planet, and for the next century nobody seriously challenged it at sea.

Historians call what followed the Pax Britannica, the British Peace. The Royal Navy policed the oceans. The pound sterling became the world's money. And Britain, the first country to industrialize, flooded the world with cheap cloth, coal and iron while hungrily importing cotton, rubber, tea and wool.

The empire also changed its mind about slavery. After decades of campaigning, much of it by formerly enslaved people themselves, Britain banned the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in most of its colonies in 1833.

But the compensation went to the slave owners, not the enslaved: twenty million pounds, around forty percent of the government's annual budget. British taxpayers only finished paying off that loan in 2015.

And free trade, it turned out, could be enforced at gunpoint. When China tried to stop British merchants from smuggling opium into the country, Britain went to war. The First Opium War ended in 1842 with China forced to open its ports and hand over a small, rocky island: Hong Kong.

## [8:00] The Jewel in the Crown

By the 1850s, the East India Company controlled most of the subcontinent.

In 1857, Indian soldiers in the Company's own army rose up. The spark was a rumor about rifle cartridges greased with cow and pig fat, offensive to Hindus and Muslims alike. The real causes ran deeper: seized land, annexed kingdoms, and a century of humiliation.

The rebellion spread across northern India. Both sides committed atrocities, and the British reprisals were ferocious. Whole villages were burned and rebels were executed in public.

When it was over, London decided a company could no longer be trusted with an empire. In 1858, the Crown took direct control. The British Raj had begun. In 1877, Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India.

India was the heart of the whole system. Its army fought Britain's wars from Africa to China. Its taxes paid for that army. Its markets bought British cloth, while its own textile industry, once the greatest in the world, withered.

And holding India meant holding the routes to India. That explains half of the rest of the map.

## [9:15] The Scramble

Look at the empire through that lens and suddenly it makes sense. Gibraltar, Malta, Cyprus, Aden: stepping stones to India. The Cape of Good Hope: the sea road around Africa.

When the Suez Canal opened in 1869, it became the empire's jugular. In 1875, Prime Minister Disraeli bought Egypt's shares in the canal, and in 1882, Britain occupied Egypt itself.

Then came the race for Africa. In 1884, European powers met in Berlin to agree the rules for carving up the continent. Not a single African was in the room.

Within a generation, almost all of Africa had been divided. Britain's share ran in a near-unbroken line from Cairo to the Cape: Egypt, Sudan, Uganda, Kenya, the Rhodesias, South Africa.

The tools were the steamship, quinine, and the machine gun. At Omdurman in Sudan, in 1898, more than ten thousand Sudanese fighters were killed in a single morning. Fewer than fifty British and Egyptian soldiers died.

But not every war was easy. In South Africa, the Boer War of 1899 to 1902 dragged on for nearly three years against the descendants of Dutch settlers. To break them, Britain burned their farms and herded civilians into concentration camps. Around twenty-eight thousand Boers died there, most of them children, and at least fourteen thousand Black Africans died in separate camps.

## [10:40] Peak empire

When the First World War broke out in 1914, the empire went to war as one. More than a million Indian soldiers served. Australians and New Zealanders died at Gallipoli. Canadians took Vimy Ridge.

And when the war ended, the empire grew again. Britain took over German colonies in Africa and Ottoman lands in the Middle East: Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan.

By the early 1920s, the British Empire covered around a quarter of the world's land and ruled roughly 450 million people. It was the largest empire in human history.

But the cracks were already showing.

In April 1919, in the city of Amritsar, British troops under General Dyer opened fire on an unarmed crowd trapped in a walled garden called Jallianwala Bagh. They fired until they ran low on ammunition. Hundreds were killed. Many Indians who had believed in British rule never believed in it again.

In 1922, after a war of independence, most of Ireland left the United Kingdom.

In 1930, a thin, sixty-year-old lawyer named Mohandas Gandhi walked about 240 miles to the sea to make salt, defying a British tax. The whole world was watching.

And in 1931, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa were recognized as fully self-governing. The empire was quietly turning into something else.

## [12:00] The war that broke the bank

The Second World War saved Britain, and it finished the empire.

Britain stood against Nazi Germany, and once again the empire stood with it. Two and a half million Indians served, the largest volunteer army in history.

But in February 1942, Singapore, the great fortress of the East, surrendered to Japan. Around eighty thousand British and imperial troops were taken prisoner. Churchill called it the worst disaster in British military history. The idea that Britain could protect its empire died that day.

In 1943, around three million people died in a famine in Bengal, made far worse by wartime decisions taken in London and Delhi.

By 1945, Britain had won, and it was broke. It owed huge debts, including to India itself, and had to borrow billions of dollars from the United States just to keep running. It paid off the last of that loan in 2006.

Britain could no longer afford an empire, and India would no longer accept one. On the fifteenth of August 1947, India and Pakistan became independent.

The border between them was drawn in about five weeks by a British lawyer who had never been to India before. Partition uprooted around fifteen million people and killed hundreds of thousands, perhaps a million or more.

The Jewel in the Crown was gone. Burma and Ceylon followed the next year, and Britain walked away from Palestine.

## [13:20] The wind of change

For a while, Britain tried to carry on as a great power.

In 1956, when Egypt's president Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal, Britain and France secretly planned an invasion with Israel to take it back. The United States was furious and put pressure on the pound. Within days, Britain backed down. The whole world saw who really held power now.

In 1957, Ghana became the first colony in sub-Saharan Africa to win independence. In 1960, Prime Minister Harold Macmillan told South Africa's parliament that "the wind of change is blowing through this continent."

Over the next decade, more than twenty colonies became independent nations: Nigeria, Jamaica, Kenya, Malaysia, Uganda, Zambia, and many more.

Not all of it was peaceful. In Kenya, during the Mau Mau uprising, Britain held tens of thousands of people in detention camps where beatings and torture were routine. In 2013, Britain finally paid compensation to more than five thousand survivors.

In 1982, Britain fought one more colonial war, sending a task force eight thousand miles to retake the Falkland Islands from Argentina.

And on the night of the thirtieth of June 1997, in pouring rain, the British flag came down over Hong Kong. The last major colony returned to China. Many people called it the end of the empire.

## [14:50] What's left

But look closely at the map today and the empire is not quite gone.

Britain still holds fourteen overseas territories: Gibraltar, Bermuda, the Falklands, tiny Pitcairn in the Pacific, and a scatter of islands across the oceans, several of them among the world's busiest tax havens.

The Commonwealth, a club of fifty-six countries, most of them former colonies, still meets. The British monarch is still head of state of Canada, Australia, New Zealand and about a dozen other countries.

English is the world's common language. Cricket is played from Lahore to Kingston. Courts from Singapore to Nairobi still use British common law.

And so do the legacies nobody chose: borders drawn with rulers across Africa and the Middle East, the scars of Partition, and arguments over treasures in British museums, and over who should apologize, and for what.

So was the British Empire a force for progress, or a vast machine for taking? Historians still fight about it, and the answer sounds very different depending on whether your ancestors lived in London, Lagos or Lucknow.

What is certain is this. For about two centuries, a small island off the coast of Europe ran a quarter of the world. And the world it left behind, its languages, its borders and its wounds, is the one we still live in.
