# Script — POV: You're a Southern Rapper New York Won't Sign in 1995

Word count: 2,550

You're 20 years old, a Black kid from East Point, Georgia, in the spring of 1995, sitting on the trunk of your uncle's '84 Cutlass Supreme in a flea market parking lot with a shoebox of homemade cassettes between your knees. Every tape has the same thing on the label. Your rap name in silver paint pen. A pager number. Five songs.

Your daddy wants you on the line at the Ford plant in Hapeville before they stop hiring. Your grandmother keeps asking when you're going back to Clark Atlanta. And every New York rapper on the radio sounds like he's from a different planet than you. But over the next 12 months, you're going to sell tapes in the worst traffic jam Atlanta has ever seen, watch a New York crowd boo two kids from your side of town, end up in a dirt-floor basement where the future of Southern rap is being built, sit across from a New York A&R who tells you to lose your accent, and hear the words "Dirty South" come out of a car speaker for the first time.

Welcome to 1995. Let's start with the night you finished your first real tape.

Your studio is a closet in your homeboy's mama's house off Washington Road. A Tascam four-track. A drum machine he bought from a pawn shop on Stewart Avenue. A mattress leaned against the wall so the neighbors don't hear the bass. That's the whole setup. You've been rapping since eighth grade. Skating rink talent shows. Lunch-table cyphers. Freestyles in the parking lot at Cascade. But this year, you decide to stop playing.

And the whole city feels like it's about to become something. Downtown, cranes are lifting steel for the Olympic stadium. Everybody's talking about 1996, about the world coming to Atlanta. Billboards. Construction. New money. You figure if the whole planet's about to show up here, somebody might as well hear you rap when they do.

So you and your partner spend three straight weekends on five songs. Two hard records. One for the clubs. One about your grandmother's kitchen. And one slow song you swear the girls are going to love. Then you run every copy yourself on a dual-deck boombox from Radio Shack. Real time. Forty-five minutes per tape. You know what making a demo means in 1995? There's no upload button. There's no link. Every single copy is a piece of plastic you dubbed by hand at two in the morning while your mama yells at you to cut that noise down. You make 200. That's two months of paychecks from the Kroger deli counter, gone.

But before you sell a single one, you try what every Southern rapper tries first. You go after New York.

Then you mail your tape north and find out what the industry really thinks of the South. The way you see it, New York is the industry. Every big label, every magazine, every A&R with real power sits somewhere between Times Square and Midtown. So you copy addresses out of the back of liner notes and mail 15 tapes. Then you wait. Weeks pass. Nothing. You call one label from a pay phone outside the Texaco, get transferred twice, and hang up when the quarters run out.

Your cousin in Brooklyn finally tells you the truth over a long-distance call that runs up your mama's phone bill. Nobody up there is listening to tapes from Georgia. To most of the industry, Southern rap means two things. Bass music and dances. Party records for the clubs and car shows. Nothing serious. Then your cousin says it straight. Y'all talk too slow. That stings. Because you know what's happening in the clubs and skating rinks down here. You know Kilo Ali can shut down a party. You know there's a whole sound growing in these neighborhoods. New York just hasn't been told yet.

So you stop mailing tapes to people who don't want them. You start selling them to people who do. And the biggest crowd you'll ever see is about to drive straight into your city.

Then Freaknik turns Atlanta into the biggest parking lot in America. April 1995. Spring break for the Black colleges, except now it's not just the AUC. Students drive in from everywhere. Florida. The Carolinas. Ohio. New Jersey. Hundreds of thousands of young Black people pour into Atlanta in one weekend. And traffic stops. Not slows down. Stops. Peachtree Street turns into a block party. People climb out of their cars. Music blasts from every trunk. And downtown sits gridlocked for hours.

You know what a traffic jam that size means to a kid with a shoebox of tapes? A captive audience. You and your partner walk between the cars all afternoon. Five dollars a tape. You play the club record off a boombox on your shoulder. Some people wave you off. Some people laugh. But a car full of girls from Tallahassee buys three. A dude from Memphis buys two and asks if you're signed. By Sunday night, you've sold 140 tapes. Your feet are destroyed. Your voice is gone. But your pager keeps going off from area codes you've never seen.

That's when you learn the first rule of the South. Nobody's going to hand you an audience. You have to walk into traffic and find one.

But one Atlanta label is already proving the whole country will buy music made here.

Meanwhile, LaFace Records shows you what Atlanta money looks like. That summer, you can't escape one song. "Waterfalls" by TLC. It's coming out of every car on Campbellton Road. Every mall. Every barbershop. On July 8th, it climbs to number one on the Billboard Hot 100 and stays there for seven straight weeks. And the company behind it isn't in New York. It's LaFace Records, the label L.A. Reid and Babyface built right here in Atlanta. TLC came up here. Jermaine Dupri is building So So Def across town. You start to realize your city already has the machinery. Writers. Producers. Executives. Studios. Atlanta has been selling hits to America for years. Just not rap hits. Not yet.

And in August, two kids from East Point are about to stand on a stage in New York and get booed for winning.

Then a VHS tape from the Source Awards changes how you see everything. August 3rd, 1995. The Source Awards at the Paramount Theater inside Madison Square Garden. You're not there. Nobody you know is there. But a week later, somebody's cousin comes back from New York with a dubbed VHS tape. And twelve of you squeeze into a living room in College Park to watch it on a 27-inch Magnavox.

Most of the night is East Coast and West Coast throwing shots at each other. Death Row against Bad Boy. Then they announce Best New Rap Group. Outkast. Andre and Big Boi. Southernplayalisticadillacmuzik. Two kids who met at Tri-Cities High School over in East Point. And when they walk up to the stage, the New York crowd boos.

The living room goes silent. Then Andre steps to the microphone and says it. The South got something to say. Somebody rewinds it. Then rewinds it again. Then again. By the fourth time, nobody's watching the TV anymore. Everybody's looking at each other. Because that line wasn't just for that crowd. It was for every rapper in Georgia who mailed a tape north and never heard back.

That night, you go home and rewrite your best verse.

But the place Outkast came out of is about ten minutes from your grandmother's house.

Then a friend of a friend gets you down into the Dungeon. Everybody in East Point has heard about it. A basement under a house owned by the mother of a producer named Rico Wade. Red clay floor. Walls covered in whatever they could find. Equipment stacked on equipment. That's where Organized Noize, Rico, Ray Murray, and Sleepy Brown made the beats behind Outkast. That's where a crew calling themselves Goodie Mob spends nights working on their own album. And the whole collective has a name. The Dungeon Family.

You get in one Saturday because your partner's cousin used to play ball with somebody in the crew. That's how it works down here. Nobody gets in off an appointment. You get in off somebody's word.

You know what hits you first? The floor is dirt. Actual Georgia clay. And the music coming out of those speakers doesn't sound like New York. It doesn't sound like Miami bass. It sounds like a church that's been running all night. Live guitar. Organ. Slow drums with room to breathe. You sit on a crate in the corner and don't say a word for two hours.

Late that night, somebody hands you a microphone and lets you rap 16 bars. Nobody claps. Nobody signs you. One guy just nods and says you're close. Close to what, you don't ask. But you walk back up those stairs knowing exactly how far you still have to go.

And two weeks later, New York finally calls back. Just not the way you wanted.

Then a New York A&R asks you to change the one thing you can't. One of your Freaknik tapes ended up with a promoter, who handed it to a DJ, who handed it to an A&R from a New York label in town for a showcase. He pages you. He wants to meet. Hotel lobby in Buckhead. Tuesday at four.

You wear your only button-down. He shows up 40 minutes late in a leather jacket, even though it's 90 degrees outside. He plays your tape on a Walkman. Skips forward. Skips again. Takes the headphones off. And he doesn't say you're bad. He says you're good. Then he says the part that ruins your whole day. Lose the accent. Rap faster. Talk less about Georgia. Nobody in Ohio knows where East Point is. Make it sound like it could come from anywhere.

You know what that really means? Make it sound like New York. He tells you to cut a new demo and send it to him in a month. Then he pays for your lemonade and walks out.

You sit in your uncle's Cutlass in that hotel parking lot for half an hour. You could do it. You've heard enough Nas and Biggie to fake it. You even try a verse on the drive home with your vowels clipped short. It sounds like somebody else. Somebody you don't know.

So you don't decide that night. You let it sit. And the answer shows up in November inside a record store.

Then Goodie Mob drops Soul Food and gives your whole region a name. November 7th, 1995. A Tuesday. The same day GZA drops Liquid Swords up in New York. You go to the record store on your lunch break and buy Soul Food on cassette, because you were in that basement and you have to hear what they finished.

It's slow. It's heavy. It's country in a way that makes no apology. Cee-Lo, Khujo, T-Mo, and Big Gipp rapping about jail, about prayer, about Southern food and Southern streets. Then you get to one track with Cool Breeze and Big Boi. "Dirty South." And the title says it all. Not "down South." Not "country." Dirty South. A name for everything you've been around your whole life, finally on a record sitting on the same racks as New York's best.

You play it three times on your uncle's tape deck in the Kroger parking lot. Then you realize something. Nobody in that basement lost their accent. Nobody rapped faster. Nobody made it sound like it could come from anywhere. They made it sound like right here. And right here is exactly what's getting heard.

You never call the New York A&R back.

But turning down New York means you still need a way to get your music out by yourself.

Then you stop waiting for somebody to sign you and start acting like the label. December 1995. You and your partner pool every dollar you've got. Leftover Freaknik money. Kroger paychecks. Two hundred dollars your grandmother hands you in an envelope without asking what it's for. You pay a duplication house to press 1,000 real cassettes. Printed labels. Shrink wrap. Your own made-up label name on the spine.

You drive them to every mom-and-pop record store on the south side and leave copies on consignment. You give a stack to the DJ at the skating rink. You hand them out at the barbershop on Saturday mornings. The first week, nothing moves. The second week, one store on the south side sells out of its ten copies and calls your pager asking for twenty more. You drive them over that same afternoon with the receipt book on the passenger seat. Then one Friday night at Cascade, the DJ drops your club record between two songs everybody already knows. Nobody stops skating. That's the best part. They just keep moving to it like it belongs there.

By March, you've sold through almost all 1,000. A local independent label calls about a single. No advance. No video budget. Just distribution in three states. You sign it at your grandmother's kitchen table.

You never become Outkast. You never get booed or cheered at Madison Square Garden. Your single sells a few thousand copies across Georgia, Alabama, and the Carolinas. You make two more records. Then, around 1999, you start spending more time behind the board than in front of the mic. You learn to engineer. For the next 25 years, you run sessions for kids who walk in with the same look you had in 1995.

And you watch what happens to your city. Outkast becomes one of the biggest groups on Earth. Atlanta stops asking New York for permission. Then it stops asking anybody. By the 2010s, the sound coming out of this city is the sound of mainstream rap, period.

One night in the fall of 2026, your nephew plays you a song he recorded on his phone in the back seat of a car. He uploaded it that morning. It already has 8,000 plays. You don't tell him he has it easy. You just ask him if the accent's his. He looks at you like it's a strange question. Of course it's his. Whose else would it be?

That's when you go out to the garage and dig through a box until you find one of the old tapes. Silver paint pen. A pager number that's been dead for 25 years. Five songs. And suddenly you're 20 again. Walking between cars on Peachtree with a boombox on your shoulder. Watching a VHS tape rewind in a living room in College Park. Sitting on a crate on a red clay floor. Wearing your only button-down in a Buckhead hotel lobby. Hearing "Dirty South" for the first time in a Kroger parking lot.

You started 1995 thinking getting signed meant getting New York to say yes. By the end of it, you'd figured out the truth. Nobody had to give you permission to be from where you're from. You just had to be good enough that strangers couldn't ignore it.
