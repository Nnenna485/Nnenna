### 1. Cold open

From space, you can see a border on this island.
On one side, green hills.
On the other, bare brown ridges.
That's strange.
It's one island.
The same rain clouds roll in off the same sea.
And many of the people on both sides arrived on the same kind of ship.
In 1960, by one IMF estimate, the average Haitian and the average Dominican earned about the same.
Today, the Dominican Republic produces about five times as much per person.
Same island.
Roughly the same number of people.
So why is Haiti poor…
but the Dominican Republic isn't?
The answer runs through rain, sugar, a bill, and a few very hard men.
And here's the twist.
Haiti didn't fall behind because it started poor.
It fell behind because it started as the richest colony on Earth, and everyone wanted their share back.
Haitians have a saying for this.
"Dèyè mòn, gen mòn."
Beyond the mountains, more mountains.

### 2. Start with the map

Start with the map.
The island is called Hispaniola, and it sits between Cuba and Puerto Rico.
In 1492, it was one of the first places Columbus landed.
Its original Taíno name was Ayiti.
Land of high mountains.
That name was earned.
The tallest peak in the whole Caribbean, Pico Duarte, stands here, over three thousand meters high.
The border cuts the island unevenly.
Haiti gets the western third.
The Dominican Republic gets the rest, almost twice the land.
And the land isn't equal either.
The trade winds blow in from the east.
They hit the Dominican mountains first and drop much of their rain there.
By the time that air crosses into Haiti, a lot of the water is gone.
Haiti is steeper, drier and rockier.
The Dominican side has the Cibao, a wide, fertile valley with big rivers running through it.
So, case closed?
Geography did it?
Not quite.
Because about two hundred and fifty years ago, the drier, steeper side was the rich one.
By far.
In the 1780s, French Saint-Domingue, today's Haiti, was the most valuable colony in the world.
By one account, it grew about forty percent of the sugar sold in Europe, and about sixty percent of the coffee.
The Spanish side next door was a backwater.
Same mountains.
Same rain.
Opposite fortunes.
Geography sets the stage.
It doesn't write the play.

### 3. The catch

But Saint-Domingue's wealth came with a catch.
It had a name: the plantation system.
And it had a rulebook, France's Code Noir, first issued in 1685.
The rulebook turned people into property.
The mechanism was simple and brutal.
Sugar needs huge amounts of labor, at harvest, in the heat, at a killing pace.
So planters bought enslaved Africans, worked them until they died, and bought more.
By 1789, there were about half a million enslaved people in the colony.
Hold on to that number.
There were only around thirty thousand white colonists.
By one account, most of the enslaved had been born in Africa, because so many died so fast.
Many newly arrived captives, by some estimates, didn't survive more than a few years.
And when coffee took off, the farms climbed the steep hills.
Forest came down from slopes too steep to hold their soil.
The first brown scars on those ridges are older than the country itself.
The colony didn't build schools or roads for the people who lived there.
It built ports.
To ship things out.

### 4. The other side

Now look east.
Spanish Santo Domingo had been Spain's first foothold in the Americas.
But Spain found far more silver in Mexico and Peru, and lost interest.
The east became cattle country.
Open ranches, small farms, and very few people.
In the 1780s, by one estimate, the whole Spanish colony had around 125,000 people.
The French side had about four times that many enslaved people alone.
Spain cared so little that, in 1795, it signed the whole colony over to France in a peace deal.
Now compare.
West: one of the richest economies on Earth, owned by a few thousand families.
East: poor, quiet and mostly ignored.
Neglect turned out to be a strange kind of protection.
With no sugar boom, the east never built a society designed only for extraction.
More people worked their own small plots.
Masters and workers often lived side by side, and mixed.
Fewer people had a reason to burn it all down.

### 6. Follow the money

So where did all that colonial wealth go?
Not into Saint-Domingue.
It sailed to France.
To the merchant ports of Bordeaux and Nantes, into townhouses, banks and shipyards.
Then, in 1791, the enslaved rose up.
Their leaders had once been enslaved themselves: Toussaint Louverture, then Jean-Jacques Dessalines.
Thirteen years of war followed.
Against the planters, then against British and Spanish invaders, then against an army sent by Napoleon.
Napoleon sent tens of thousands of troops to restore slavery.
Between rebel armies and yellow fever, most of them never went home.
On the first of January 1804, Haiti declared independence.
It was the first country born from a successful slave revolt.
Remember that half a million?
They had won.
But the plantations were burned, the planters were gone, and the great powers wouldn't trade normally with a nation of freed slaves.
Haiti's new rulers tried forcing people back onto the plantations to restart the sugar machine.
It never came back.
The United States didn't recognize Haiti until 1862.
And France wanted to be paid.

### 7. The set-piece: 1825

In July 1825, a French squadron sailed into the harbor at Port-au-Prince.
By one account, more than a dozen warships, guns facing the capital.
The envoy carried an order from King Charles the Tenth.
France would recognize Haiti's independence.
On one condition.
Haiti would pay 150 million francs to compensate the former colonists for their lost property.
The property was the people who had freed themselves.
President Jean-Pierre Boyer had a choice.
Pay, or face war and blockade.
Eight days later, he accepted.
Haiti didn't have the money.
So it borrowed the first installment from a French bank, at steep fees.
A loan, to pay a ransom, owed to the same country.
Historians call it the double debt.
In 1838, France cut the total.
It barely helped.
By one New York Times investigation, Haiti ended up paying about 112 million francs over roughly seventy years.
To find that money, the government taxed its coffee farmers, again and again.
Money that could have built schools, roads and courts went to Paris instead.
The same investigation put the cost in lost growth at somewhere between twenty-one and a hundred and fifteen billion dollars.
Not stolen.
Never earned.
And the strangest twist?
That same investigation found that a French bank which later profited from Haiti's loans also helped finance the Eiffel Tower.

### 8. Boom and bust

From 1822 to 1844, Haiti even ruled the whole island.
Which is why the Dominican Republic's independence day celebrates breaking away from Haiti, not from Spain.
After that, Haiti's history fell into a pattern.
A coffee boom brought money in.
The money went out in debt payments.
Rival generals fought over what was left.
A coup.
Then another.
Between 1911 and 1915 alone, Haiti went through about seven presidents.
In December 1914, American Marines walked into Haiti's national bank and carried off half a million dollars in gold.
It was shipped to a bank in New York.
Seven months later, in 1915, the Marines landed for real, and stayed for nineteen years.
They took control of Haiti's customs and its debts.
The Dominican Republic was occupied too, from 1916 to 1924.
Then both sides got dictators.
Rafael Trujillo ruled the Dominican Republic from 1930 to 1961.
François Duvalier, Papa Doc, took Haiti in 1957, then passed it to his son.
In 1964, he declared himself president for life.
Both were brutal.
But they were different kinds of brutal.
Trujillo stole on an industrial scale, but he built roads, ports and a state that outlived him.
The Duvaliers ruled through a private militia, the Tonton Macoutes, and let the state itself rot.
Tens of thousands were killed, by most estimates, and much of Haiti's educated class left the country.
Doctors, teachers and engineers went to Montreal, Miami and Paris.
By 1986, when Baby Doc finally fled, the country had lost a generation of the people who run things.
After Trujillo was shot in 1961, the Dominican Republic went through a coup, a civil war and an American invasion.
Then, from 1966, it settled into elections.
Imperfect.
But continuous.
It built hotels, free-trade zones and factories.
In recent years, it has drawn roughly eight to eleven million visitors a year.
It had crashes too, including a huge banking collapse in 2003.
But it kept getting back up.
Over the last half century, it has been one of the fastest-growing economies in Latin America.
Haiti's crashes kept getting worse.
Coups in 1991 and 2004.
Hurricanes.
Floods that tore the soil off those bare hills.
Then came the worst of all.
On the twelfth of January 2010, an earthquake struck just outside Port-au-Prince.
Haitians gave it a name: Goudougoudou, for the sound the ground made.
Estimates of the dead run from about 100,000 to over 300,000.
In 2021, Haiti's president was assassinated in his own bedroom.
Since then, armed gangs have taken over most of the capital.

### 9. Back to the anchor

So, back to the mountains.
Dèyè mòn, gen mòn.
Beyond the mountains, more mountains.
For Haiti, the mountains kept coming.
A plantation system built to work people to death.
A war for freedom.
A bill for that freedom.
Occupation, dictatorship, earthquake, gangs.
Every time it crossed one ridge, the next one was already there.
And on the other side of the border?
Fewer mountains.
Not none.
Fewer.

### 10. The honest part

But here's the honest part.
The gap wasn't destiny.
In 1960, by that IMF estimate, the two countries were still level.
Most of the divergence happened within a single lifetime.
And those brown hills from the start?
They're as much a result of poverty as a cause.
Families cut trees for charcoal because there was nothing else to cook with.
And the Dominican Republic is no fairy tale.
In 1937, Trujillo's soldiers massacred somewhere between twelve and thirty thousand Haitians along the border.
And its success still leans on Haitian hands: hundreds of thousands of Haitian workers cut its cane and build its hotels.
Haiti has had its escapes, too.
In the 1970s and '80s, by one account, factories in Port-au-Prince stitched nearly every baseball used in the American major leagues.
And today, Haitians abroad send home money worth about a fifth of the whole economy.
The talent was never the problem.

### 11. The scorecard

So, the scorecard.
Geography: the east got more rain, more flat land and twice the space.
Colonial inheritance: the west got the richest plantations on Earth, and the deepest scars.
Debt: Haiti paid for its own freedom for about seventy years, and the east never got that bill.
Politics: both had tyrants, but only one kept a working state after its tyrant was gone.

### 12. Verdict

So why did the Dominican Republic pull ahead while Haiti fell behind?
Not because of the soil.
Not because of the people.
Because Haiti kept being made to pay for being valuable.

### 13. Closing line

The richest colony on Earth didn't become poor despite its wealth.
It became poor because of it.
